# Autokinesis reveals a threshold for perception of visual motion

## Abstract

In natural viewing conditions, the brain can optimally integrate retinal and extraretinal signals to maintain a stable visual perception. These mechanisms, however, may fail in circumstances where extraction of a motion signal is less viable such as impoverished visual scenes. This can result in a phenomenon known as autokinesis in which one may experience apparent motion of a small visual stimulus in an otherwise completely dark environment. In this study, we examined the effect of autokinesis on visual perception of motion in human observers. We used a novel method with optical tracking in which the visual motion was reported manually by the observer. Experiment results show at lower speeds of motion, the perceived direction of motion was more aligned with the effect of autokinesis, whereas in the light or at higher speeds in the dark, it was more aligned with the actual direction of motion. These findings have important implications for understanding how the stability of visual representation in the brain can affect accurate perception of motion signals.

## Introduction

A remarkable aspect of the brain function is the ability to maintain a stable perception of the world as the visual inputs on the retina alter due to continuous changes in the eye, head, and body positions. In natural viewing conditions, the brain can optimally integrate retinal and extraretinal sensory signals to maintain a stable perceptual representation of the surrounding. These mechanisms, however, may fail in circumstances where extraction of a motion signal is less viable such as impoverished visual scenes. This can result in a phenomenon known as autokinesis in which observers experience an apparent motion of a stationary visual stimulus in an otherwise completely dark environment (Carr, 1910; Royce et al., 1966; Levy, 1972).

The effect of autokinesis varies among individuals, but generally the perceived motion increases with time and it can be also influenced by the shape, size, and distance of the visual stimulus, the level of illumination, or the presence of visual cues (Adams, 1912; Graybiel and Clark, 1945; Edwards, 1954; Luchins, 1954; Gregory and Zangwill, 1963; Levy, 1972). It has long been debated whether autokinesis originates from changes in the eye position, as the movement of the eye is accompanied by corresponding changes in the position of the retinal image (Guilford and Dallenbach, 1928; Skolnick, 1940; Gibson, 1954; Gregory and Zangwill, 1963; Matin and Mackinnon, 1964; Lehman, 1965; Galletti and Fattori, 2003; Murakami, 2004; Poletti et al., 2010). This extraretinal signal could originate from the eye proprioceptors or from the motor command itself, which is known as the efference copy or corollary discharge. The brain can also infer movement of the eye when images change position on the retina. The specific mechanisms by which the brain uses these retinal and extraretinal contributions to maintain visual stability remain a matter of investigation. Prior studies have found no correlation between eye movement and perceived motion during autokinesis(Gregory and Zangwill, 1963). These findings suggest that the visual system must rely on both retinal and extraretinal contributions and use the retinal stimulus to discard visual changes caused by the movement of the eye (Poletti et al., 2010). This mechanism, however, may fail when there is a lack of visual cues to establish the motion of the retinal image and discount those changes induced by eye movement (i.e., autokinesis) (Poletti et al., 2010).

While prior investigations have focused on the retinal and extraretinal mechanisms for autokinesis, the direct effect of autokinesis on visual perception of motion was not examined previously. That is, whether autokinesis can alter how the motion of the visual stimulus is perceived while it is moving. To address this question, here we used a novel method with optical tracking in which observers could manually report their perceived movement of a visual stimulus. The effect of autokinesis was measured with viewing of the stimulus when it was stationary in an otherwise completely dark room. We first examined whether the pattern of perceived motion during autokinesis was consistent among individual observers. The result was then compared with perceived motion of the visual stimulus at different speeds in the dark, and when it was moving in the light with visual cues present.

## Experimental Procedures

### Participants

Twelve healthy subjects (age 32.2±9.5, 5 females) participated in this study. All subjects except one were right-handed by self-report and all were screened to exclude history of visual, neurologic, or psychiatric illness. The experiments were approved by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board and informed written consent was obtained from all subjects.

### Setup

Subjects sat upright in a room that was specially designed to perform experiments in the dark. There was no window, the doors were light-sealed, and the walls, floor, and the ceiling were all in black color. The visual stimulus was presented on a CRT monitor (1600 pixels by 1200 pixels) which was placed 135 cm in front of the subject. The center of the screen was aligned at the eye level for individual subjects. The screen frame was covered by a black board with a circular opening (diameter of 11.8°), and the brightness and contrast levels were set to the minimum at the luminance less than 0.5 cd/m^2.

A black board (99 cm by 103 cm) was installed 20 cm in front of the subject, and it was aligned so that the right hand could be placed at the center of the board. A reflective rigid body marker that could be moved freely on the board was used by the subject to report perceived motion in parallel to the CRT screen (Figure 1; subject marker). The marker was tracked by an infrared optical tracker (Polaris Vicra, NDI, Waterloo, Ontario, Canada) with submillimeter accuracy. The marker position was transformed into the board’s frame of reference by using another rigid body marker attached to the board and also tracked by the infrared camera (Figure 1; base rigid body). The data acquisition rate ranged from 4.5 to 12.5 Hz and was interpolated to ensure equal time intervals for data analysis.

### Task and procedure

The tasks consisted of moving the marker manually on the board to report perceived motion of a visual dot (0.21° diameter) on the CRT screen. Subjects were monitored remotely through an infrared camera to ensure that the instructions were followed correctly during the tasks. Two different tasks were used to evaluate (i) the perceived motion of a static dot (i.e., autokinesis) and (ii) the perceived motion of a moving dot. The orders of recordings were counterbalanced among the subjects to avoid any order effect from performing the tasks. The subjects had no prior knowledge of the movement of the dot in both task. They were instructed to move the marker at the same speed and direction as they perceived the movement of the dot in front of them. At each trial, subjects held the marker on the board at a starting location within their comfortable reach.

#### Static dot:

In this task, first a white dot appeared on the screen for 20 seconds before it turned red along with an auditory cue to start the trial. The red dot remained stationary at the center of the screen for 5 minutes. The auditory cue along with a change of the dot color to white indicated the end of the recording block. The recording was repeated once after a 5-minute wash-out period with the light on. This second block was also recorded for 5 minutes to examine whether the pattern of motion perceived during autokinesis was consistent with repeated measurements in individual subjects. We used RealEyes xDVR system (Micromedical Technologies Inc., Chatham, IL, USA) and a custom software to track horizontal and vertical eye positions in the static task. This system uses two cameras mounted on goggles to capture infrared images of each eye(Otero-Millan et al., 2015). To calibrate eye position, the average values of 12 mm was used as the radius of the globe and 6 mm as the radius of the iris (i.e., displacement of the pupil equal to the radius of the iris corresponds to 30° eye movement) (Otero-Millan et al., 2015).

#### Moving dot:

Like the static task, a white dot appeared on the screen for 20 seconds after which it turned red with an auditory cue as a signal to start the trial. The red dot moved from the center of the screen in one of the four orthogonal directions (right, left, up or down) for a distance of 4.9° in all trials. At the end of each trial, the white dot reappeared at the center of the screen along with an auditory cue to indicate the end of the trial. The subject then moved the marker back to a starting point on the board in front of them before the next trial started. To prevent any order effect or prediction of motion direction, a total of six trials in random order were used (four orthogonal directions and two randomly selected orthogonal directions). There was a total of five blocks, each with the dot moving at a different speed (i.e., a total of 5 blocks each with 6 trials). The base speed was at 0.16 °/s (speed x) in which it took 30.10 seconds for the dot to move across the screen. The speeds used in the other blocks were 2, 3, 4, and 6 times as fast, in which it took 15.27s (speed of 2x), 9.90 s (speed 3x), 7.37s (speed 4x), and 4.93s (speed 6x) for the dot to move across the screen at each trial. Subjects were dark adapted for 20s before the start of each block, and the lights were turned on between the blocks. Another block was also recorded with the dot moving at the base speed (i.e., 0.16 °/s or speed x) with the light on. The order of recording blocks was counterbalanced among the subjects.

### Data analysis

The motion path recorded from each task was converted to direction vectors by connecting the start and end positions within the time intervals of 0.2 seconds. The distribution of direction vectors was derived as their percentage (or density) within 16 angle bins of 22.5° (Figure 2). In the task with the moving dot, the difference between the actual and perceived motion directions was used to calculate the direction index (d): (1) d=∑i=1Ncos(θi−θo)N

In equation (1), θi is the vector angle of the ith direction vector, θo is the actual moving angle of the dot (i.e., an orthogonal direction), and N is the total number of the direction vectors in the corresponding trial. Based on this equation, a larger d (range −1 to 1) indicates a better alignment between the actual and perceived motion directions. To examine whether the perceived motion directions correspond with autokinesis, the average density of the vectors in the task with the moving dot was derived (p), using the distribution of the vectors in the static task: (2) p=∑Ni=1bi(k)/bmaxN

In equation (2), bi(k) is the density of the kth bin from the task with the static dot, where the ith vector from the task with the moving dot falls in, and N is the total number of direction vectors in the corresponding trial. In this equation, the b was normalized using its highest value for each subject (bmax).

## Results

All subjects reported autokinesis during 5min viewing of the static dot in the dark. This is shown as the polar distribution of the direction vectors for individual subjects in Figure 2.

The effect of autokinesis was consistent when the recording was repeated in the static task, with an overall significant correlation of the motion paths (R^2: 0.610, p-value: 0.002) between both blocks of recording in individual subjects (Figure 3). There was also no significant difference in the distribution of direction vectors between the two blocks (two-tailed t-test, p-value 1.000). On average, the most common vectors were those in the bin angles that corresponded with horizontal and vertical motions (i.e., bin angles −180°/left, −90°/ down, 0°/right, and 90°/up; Figure 2B). The eye position in the static task was on average close to the fixation point during autokinesis, and overall, there was no clear association between the changes in the eye position and perceived direction of motion among subjects (Supplementary figures 1 & 2).

With the viewing of the moving dot, the vectors derived at the low speed in the light were accurate with a high d index (speed x light; left 0.88±0.02, right 0.89±0.01, up 0.85±0.02, down 0.86±0.02). The accuracy, however, dropped to a low d index at the same speed in the dark (speed x; left 0.02±0.11, right 0.36±0.11, up 0.25±0.16, down 0.28±0.11). The d index increased subsequently at the higher speeds in the dark, showing a better alignment between the perceived and actual directions of motion (Two-way Repeated ANOVA; speed effect, p-value <0.001) (Figure 4).

With the consistent pattern of autokinesis in individual subjects (Figure 3), the distribution of the vectors from the static task was compared with the vectors derived from the perceived motion of the moving dot in individual subjects (Figure 5).

Consistent with the d index results, there were fewer vectors that aligned with the actual direction of motion at the low speed in the dark compared with the higher speeds in the dark or the low speed in the light. The percentage of the vectors that did not fall within the same bin as the actual motion direction were 78%±2 at speed x, 69%±4 at speed 2x, 61%±3 at speed 3x, 60%±4 at speed 4x, 60%±4 at speed 6x; and 34%±2 at speed x light. From these vectors, the density of the motion vectors that matched the autokinesis map was significantly different across all speeds (p index; speed x 0.44±0.03, speed 2× 0.38±0.04, speed 3× 0.29±0.02, speed 4× 0.29±0.03, speed 6× 0.26±0.03, speed x in light 0.23±0.02; One-way Repeated ANOVA; p-value <0.001). That is, there was a larger effect of autokinesis at the low speed of motion in the dark compared with the higher speeds in the dark or with the low speed in the light (Figure 6). This result is consistent with a better alignment of the perceived motion directions with autokinesis at lower speeds of motion in the dark.

## Discussion

In this study, we measured autokinesis as an apparent motion of a static visual stimulus. In line with previous reports, there was a consistent pattern of perceived motion directions with repeated recordings of autokinesis in individual observers (Pearce, 1966; Wieland and Mefferd, 1966). On this basis, the effect of autokinesis on perceived motion could be examined during actual motion of visual stimulus. Our data indicate that autokinesis may affect how the moving visual stimulus is perceived and this effect is dependent on the speed of motion. At lower speeds, the perceived motion direction was more aligned with autokinesis, whereas at higher speeds it was more aligned with the actual direction of motion. These results show that the effect of autokinesis was more pronounced when extraction of a motion signal was less viable in an impoverished visual scene. That is, the motion of the stimulus was accurately perceived when it moved under the light illumination, whereas in the dark, the probability of reporting visual motion based on autokinesis increased with the ambiguity of the motion signal (i.e., the higher effect of autokinesis at the lower speed of motion). This finding is compatible with previous studies that show visual stability is maintained based on how motion is determined on the retina (Poletti et al., 2010). Accordingly, any information available through the retinal inputs can be used by the brain to override an inherent instability in visual perception that may otherwise result in autokinesis. This information can be in the form of reliability of the motion signal or presence of visual references in the natural environment.

Both retinal and extraretinal mechanisms have been proposed to account for how the brain maintains visual stability (Guilford and Dallenbach, 1928; Skolnick, 1940; Gibson, 1954; Gregory and Zangwill, 1963; Matin and Mackinnon, 1964; Lehman, 1965; Murakami, 2004; Poletti et al., 2010). The extraretinal contribution reflects the effect of small or large eye movement, where the motor or sensory signals that encode the position of the eye must be accounted for to correct any visual changes caused by eye movements. The retinal mechanism, on the other hand, must rely on the visual input itself to correct any changes in the retinal image caused by the movement of the eye. The contribution from the retinal mechanism is supported by a visual phenomenon known as the waterfall illusion, when an apparent motion of a static visual stimulus can be induced after viewing a moving stimulus in the absence of eye movement (Mather et al., 2008; Wade, 2018). In natural conditions, the brain may rely on both retinal and extraretinal contributions to determine the movement of a visual stimulus with respect to the eyes. These mechanisms, however, may not interact efficiently in the settings like impoverished visual scenes where the accurate estimation of the motion signal on the retina is not viable (Poletti et al., 2010). Such uncertainty prevents discrimination of whether the motion signal is originated from the changes in the eye position or from the changes in the visual scene. In line with this notion, the autokinetic effect is known to be influenced by illumination of the visual stimulus or presence of additional stimuli in the visual field (Luchins, 1954; Pearce, 1966; Post et al., 1982; Ramachandran et al., 2018).

Here we used a novel method based on optical tracking to manually record perceived motion during autokinesis. In line with findings from previous studies, there was no clear association between the changes in the eye position and perceived direction of motion during autokinesis among subjects ^2,15. When motion is reported manually, a motor bias from drawing the path of motion or muscle fatigue can be mixed with perceptual responses. As a result, the size of displacement derived from the speed and duration of perceived motion may not be an accurate measure of autokinesis (Pearce, 1966; Wieland and Mefferd, 1966; Levy, 1972; Shoemaker and Bolt, 1992). In addition, the visual stimulus may seem to be moving at various speeds during autokinesis, while physically not changing position with respect to the observer. Such paradoxical motion could make any physical estimate of the size of the movement ambiguous. Thus, the apparent displacement reported manually may not directly reflect the size of motion as perceived during autokinesis. In this context, the direction of perceived motion is a more reliable measure of autokinesis (Levy, 1972). Likewise, here we used the perceived motion directions to measure autokinesis. Our results show that the distribution of perceived motion directions was reproducible with repeated measurements in the static task (Figure 3). Consistent with previous reports (Levy, 1972), our findings show that the apparent motion from autokinesis was mainly in orthogonal directions (Figure 2).

Overall, our results show that an inherent visual instability caused by autokinesis can affect how the actual motion direction is perceived. This effect was dependent on the ambiguity of the motion signal, suggesting a threshold at which the visual system can accurately perceive motion signals. At a low speed, the perceived motion was more aligned with the effect of autokinesis, but as the speed increased, it became more aligned with the actual motion direction. The presence of threshold for motion detection is in line with the pattern of cortical neural activity observed in response to moving visual stimuli (Riva Sanseverino et al., 1979). This result has important implications for understanding how the stability of visual representation within the brain can affect perception of motion, and how their interaction can be used to determine a threshold for accurate detection of visual motion.

Both retinal and extraretinal mechanisms have been proposed to account for how the brain maintains visual stability (Guilford and Dallenbach, 1928; Skolnick, 1940; Gibson, 1954; Gregory and Zangwill, 1963; Matin and Mackinnon, 1964; Lehman, 1965; Murakami, 2004; Poletti et al., 2010). The extraretinal contribution reflects the effect of small or large eye movement, where the motor or sensory signals that encode the position of the eye must be accounted for to correct any visual changes caused by eye movements. The retinal mechanism, on the other hand, must rely on the visual input itself to correct any changes in the retinal image caused by the movement of the eye. The contribution from the retinal mechanism is supported by a visual phenomenon known as the waterfall illusion, when an apparent motion of a static visual stimulus can be induced after viewing a moving stimulus in the absence of eye movement (Mather et al., 2008; Wade, 2018). In natural conditions, the brain may rely on both retinal and extraretinal contributions to determine the movement of a visual stimulus with respect to the eyes. These mechanisms, however, may not interact efficiently in the settings like impoverished visual scenes where the accurate estimation of the motion signal on the retina is not viable (Poletti et al., 2010). Such uncertainty prevents discrimination of whether the motion signal is originated from the changes in the eye position or from the changes in the visual scene. In line with this notion, the autokinetic effect is known to be influenced by illumination of the visual stimulus or presence of additional stimuli in the visual field (Luchins, 1954; Pearce, 1966; Post et al., 1982; Ramachandran et al., 2018).

Here we used a novel method based on optical tracking to manually record perceived motion during autokinesis. In line with findings from previous studies, there was no clear association between the changes in the eye position and perceived direction of motion during autokinesis among subjects ^2,15. When motion is reported manually, a motor bias from drawing the path of motion or muscle fatigue can be mixed with perceptual responses. As a result, the size of displacement derived from the speed and duration of perceived motion may not be an accurate measure of autokinesis (Pearce, 1966; Wieland and Mefferd, 1966; Levy, 1972; Shoemaker and Bolt, 1992). In addition, the visual stimulus may seem to be moving at various speeds during autokinesis, while physically not changing position with respect to the observer. Such paradoxical motion could make any physical estimate of the size of the movement ambiguous. Thus, the apparent displacement reported manually may not directly reflect the size of motion as perceived during autokinesis. In this context, the direction of perceived motion is a more reliable measure of autokinesis (Levy, 1972). Likewise, here we used the perceived motion directions to measure autokinesis. Our results show that the distribution of perceived motion directions was reproducible with repeated measurements in the static task (Figure 3). Consistent with previous reports (Levy, 1972), our findings show that the apparent motion from autokinesis was mainly in orthogonal directions (Figure 2).

Overall, our results show that an inherent visual instability caused by autokinesis can affect how the actual motion direction is perceived. This effect was dependent on the ambiguity of the motion signal, suggesting a threshold at which the visual system can accurately perceive motion signals. At a low speed, the perceived motion was more aligned with the effect of autokinesis, but as the speed increased, it became more aligned with the actual motion direction. The presence of threshold for motion detection is in line with the pattern of cortical neural activity observed in response to moving visual stimuli (Riva Sanseverino et al., 1979). This result has important implications for understanding how the stability of visual representation within the brain can affect perception of motion, and how their interaction can be used to determine a threshold for accurate detection of visual motion.
